Contractor overcharging in the UK — what you can do

If a contractor's final bill is significantly higher than the original quote, you may not have to pay the full amount. Your rights depend on whether you were given a fixed quote or an estimate — and whether the extra charges were agreed.

5 min read

Quote vs estimate — a crucial difference

A quote is a fixed price offer. If you accept it, the contractor is bound by that price — they cannot charge more unless additional work was specifically agreed in writing.

An estimate is an approximate figure, not a fixed price. A contractor can charge more than an estimate — but the final charge must still be reasonable. Under the Supply of Goods and Services Act 1982, where no price is fixed, the charge must be reasonable.

When you can dispute the final bill

  • The final bill exceeds a fixed written quote without any agreed variation
  • Additional charges were added without your knowledge or consent
  • The work was not completed to a satisfactory standard (Consumer Rights Act 2015)
  • Materials used were different from those quoted without prior agreement
  • The estimate was significantly exceeded without any warning or explanation
Get changes in writing If a contractor identifies additional work mid-job, always agree the extra cost in writing before they proceed. A verbal agreement is harder to prove if there is a later dispute.

How to dispute an overcharge

Write to the contractor setting out specifically which charges you are disputing and why. Reference the original quote or estimate. State the amount you are willing to pay and give a reasonable deadline for a response. Keep all written records.

If the dispute cannot be resolved directly, you can escalate to the contractor's trade association (if they are a member), a certified Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) scheme, or as a last resort the small claims court for amounts up to £10,000.
